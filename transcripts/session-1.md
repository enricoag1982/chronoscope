### Claude

Short answer: I'd skip Streamlit for this one.

**Why it's a bad fit here specifically**

1. Streamlit reruns your whole script on every widget change. The heart of this demo is dragging a time cursor and watching the balance recompute — that needs to feel instant, and Streamlit gives you a network round trip plus a full rerender per pixel of slider travel.
2. Two synchronized custom timelines — events as dots, slanted arrows connecting event time to arrival time, a moving cursor, shaded "known / not yet known" regions — is hand-drawn vector graphics. In Streamlit you'd be fighting Altair or Plotly to approximate it. Drawing it directly is less work and looks better.
3. Streamlit Community Cloud sleeps idle apps. Your interviewer clicks the link and stares at a boot screen for 30 seconds. A static page loads instantly and can't break.

**What I'd build instead**

One static page — no server at all. The entire bitemporal engine is maybe 120 lines of pure logic that runs in the browser. Deploy it to GitHub Pages, Netlify, or Vercel; it's a drag-and-drop or a single command, and there's nothing to keep alive.

The part that reads as backend engineering stays yours: a single pure module (`bitemporal.ts`) holding the event log as records of `(eventTime, arrivalTime, amount)`, with one function `balanceAt(eventTime, knownAt)`, plus a small unit test suite. That file is what an interviewer actually reads to judge you. The drawing code around it is scaffolding, and the agent writes it.

If you want Python in the stack for credibility, the honest version is a FastAPI service exposing that same query, with the page calling it. It costs you a second deployment and a cold start, and it buys you nothing the interviewer can see. I'd only do it if you want to claim "the engine is a real service."

**One suggestion on the concept itself**

Your bullet list has a single slider over event time. That shows half of bitemporality. The idea only really lands with two independent cursors:

- *event time* — when did it happen
- *arrival time* — what did we know, and when

Then you can ask the question that makes the concept click: "what did we believe the balance was on March 5th, if you asked us on March 3rd?" versus "…if you asked us today?" Same event time, different answers. Your late refund becomes a visible rectangular region on the event-time × arrival-time plane where belief and truth disagree, and it collapses the moment the refund arrives.

Worth adding a second kind of late event too — a retroactive *correction* to an existing transaction, not just a late insert. Seeing the difference between "we didn't know yet" and "we knew, but we were wrong" is the thing most people never get from a static explanation.

Yes — I'd switch. HR is the better demo, for three reasons that aren't just familiarity:

**1. Backdating is normal there, not a failure.** A bank refund arrives late because something lagged — it reads as a system defect you're working around. An HR promotion approved March 20 *effective January 1* is nobody's bug; it's how the domain works. That reframes bitemporality as modelling reality rather than patching latency, which is the more honest lesson.

**2. You get both directions.** Bank events can only be late. HR facts can be backdated *or* announced ahead — a raise recorded in December, effective January 1. So the plane fills out as a full rectangle: retroactive on one side of the diagonal, proactive on the other, with "recorded exactly when it took effect" as the diagonal itself. My earlier triangular plane was a consequence of the banking domain, and losing it is an upgrade — two quadrants teach more than one.

**3. Corrections are natural.** The plan needs a "we knew, and we were wrong" event. Correcting a bank transaction is artificial — real ledgers post a reversing entry instead, they never amend. "HR typed the wrong salary band and fixed it two weeks later" needs no justification.

**Scenario I'd use** — one employee, with a small team behind her for the aggregate:

| Recorded | Effective | Fact |
|---|---|---|
| Jan 1 | Jan 1 | Hired, 60k, Engineer |
| Feb 10 | Feb 1 | Raise to 65k *(mildly retroactive — routine)* |
| Mar 20 | Jan 1 | Promotion, Senior Engineer, 75k *(backdated a quarter — back pay)* |
| Apr 5 | Jan 1 | Correction: that was a typo, 78k |
| Apr 10 | Jun 1 | Promotion effective next quarter *(proactive)* |

"What was Alice paid in February?" has a different answer depending on the day you ask, and the app makes you feel that rather than read it.

**The aggregate gets better too.** First-quarter payroll, as reported in April, versus the same query re-run today. That's a restatement with real consequences — back pay, amended tax filings — and it's a far more grown-up motivation than a wrong account balance.

**On reporting structure specifically: I'd leave it out.** "Who did Alice report to on March 1, as far as we knew on February 15?" is the most vivid question in this whole space, but a tree layout with animated transitions is easily an hour on its own, and you lose the aggregate-over-time story that makes the chart work. Cheap compromise: show current manager as a plain text field that flips as you drag the cursors. Costs nothing and proves the point that bitemporality isn't only about numbers.

One naming note: the standard terms are *valid time* and *transaction time*, but I'd label the axes **effective** and **recorded** in the interface and mention the formal pair once in a footnote. The demo should teach the concept before the vocabulary.

### Claude

Architecturally yes — every strategy is a pure function of the fact log, and nothing is keyed to the scenario. But the plan as written under-delivers on it: I scoped the add-fact form as a minor control and listed it *first* on the cut list. If arbitrary injection is a real goal rather than a nicety, four things need to change.

**1. System time cannot be arbitrary.** This is the one that actually matters. System time is append-only — you can never record a fact at a system time earlier than something already recorded, because that axis *is* the audit history. If the form lets you set it freely, users can build histories no real store could produce, and the cost comparison stops meaning anything: "records touched by a retroactive correction" depends entirely on system-time ordering.

So the form takes a valid time freely — that's the interesting axis — and records at *now*, where "now" is a clock the user can advance. Any history remains constructible by stepping the clock between insertions, and the invariant survives.

**2. The strategies have to be fuzzed, not just checked against the scenario.** A property sweep over one fixed log will not catch an `apply()` that quietly assumes the scenario's shape. The fix is generated logs — random facts with monotonic system time — materialized under all five strategies and compared against the oracle at every coordinate. That is what makes arbitrary injection safe rather than nominally supported, and "I fuzzed five representations against a reference implementation" is a better line in an interview than any panel in the interface.

**3. Snapshot interval becomes a control.** Otherwise injected data never exercises the snapshot strategies interestingly — the whole invalidation story is a function of that parameter.

**4. Reset and undo, and bounded rendering.** People will inject junk and need out. Twenty facts pushes the plane to a few hundred rectangles, which is fine, but the snapshot stack and interval list need to scroll rather than blow out the layout.
